We tested one frozen PIP-Net on twenty bird photos from ten species. It adds up learned visual clues, called prototypes.

We swapped head with tail, or wing with tail, adding twenty-four pixels of separation. We measured raw evidence for the true species, not probability.

In photo ten, either swap cut evidence from about thirty-two to below one.

But in photo twelve, swapping wing and tail raised evidence from nineteen to twenty-four. One prototype's contribution nearly vanished; others contributed more. The prediction stayed correct, with different support.

Across twenty photos, the head-tail swap lowered evidence every time. The wing-tail swap lowered it in fourteen and raised it in six.

We also tested sixteen combinations of four parts using second-order Shapley-Taylor analysis to measure how parts work together. Head-wing interaction scores usually decreased. Lower signed scores don't always mean weaker effects, and these scores include effects from other parts.

Moving the whole bird also changed scores. Position and visible editing seams matter, so these results describe our edits to this model.
